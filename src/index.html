Question 1

Conserving and restoring natural resources

Strategies for three resources every ecosystem needs: forests, soil and water.

Question 1: Forests

Replant the slopes

Roots hold soil in place, and leaves soften the rain. Take the trees away and the hillside washes into the river.

Strategies

  • Plant a mix of native species. Local wildlife depends on them, and a mixed forest copes better with disease.
  • Protect natural regrowth. Fence out grazing animals so surviving seeds and stumps can regrow.
  • Harvest sustainably. Take no more timber than the forest can replace.

42%

of China's Loess Plateau study area was eroded before restoration, and about 27% after. Farmland on steep slopes was returned to vegetation from 1999. Zhao 2020

Question 1: Soil

Keep the soil on the land

Soil forms slowly, but one storm can strip it from bare ground. Keeping it covered and slowing the water protects it.

Strategies

  • Contour ploughing: furrows follow the shape of the slope and slow runoff. It can cut soil loss by up to 50% on moderate slopes. USDA
  • Terraces: steps cut into steep slopes hold soil and water.
  • Less tillage and mulch: roots and straw left in place protect the soil.
  • Coir or jute mats: natural fibres hold steep ground while seedlings root, then rot away.
Question 1: Water

Give the river room

Wetlands slow water down. Mud settles out, plants and microbes take up extra nutrients, and floods are soaked up.

Strategies

  • Rebuild reed beds where field drains meet the river. Bacteria on the roots turn nitrate into nitrogen gas.
  • Plant buffer strips of trees and grasses along the banks to trap runoff and shade the water.
  • Use less water. Drip irrigation and rain tanks leave more in the river during dry months.

Why restoring wetlands matters

How fertiliser kills fish

  1. A healthy lake. Light reaches the bottom. Water plants make oxygen, and fish breathe it.
  2. Nutrients wash in, and algae bloom. Spare nitrate and phosphate from fields and sewage feed algae, which cover the surface.
  3. The light goes out. Plants die in the shade. Bacteria feeding on the dead matter use up the dissolved oxygen.
  4. Fish suffocate. This is eutrophication. The fish die from lack of oxygen, even though the water isn't toxic.
  5. Stop the source, and it recovers. A reed bed and buffer strip catch the nutrients. Recovery can take years.

Question 2

Reducing pollution

Strategies for air, water and land. Preventing pollution works better than cleaning it up later.

Question 2: Air pollution

Clean the smoke, then replace the fuel

Burning coal releases sulphur dioxide, which forms acid rain. Acid rain damages leaves and strips nutrients from soil.

Strategies

  • Fit scrubbers. Wet limestone reacts with sulphur dioxide and traps it as gypsum before it leaves the stack.
  • Switch to solar and wind. They make electricity with no smoke, and they cut carbon dioxide too.
  • Clean up transport. Catalytic converters, electric vehicles, buses and cycling cut exhaust fumes.

drop in sulphur dioxide from US power plants, 1990 to 2019, under emission limits and pollution trading. US EPA

Question 2: Water pollution

Treat it before it reaches the river

Watch the brown water clear as the treatment tanks come online. Clean water follows the current downstream.

Strategies

  • Treat wastewater. Solids settle out, bacteria digest organic waste, and a final stage removes leftover nutrients and metals.
  • Clean up past pollution. Bioremediation uses bacteria to break down oil, and plants to draw metals from soil.
  • Keep it out of drains. Never pour oil, paint or medicine down the sink.

0+

fish species recorded in the tidal Thames since 1957, the year it was declared biologically dead. Sewage treatment made that return possible. ZSL

Question 2: Land pollution

Shrink the dump

Burning rubbish fouls the air, and rain draining through it carries toxic liquid into the groundwater.

Strategies, best first

  1. Refuse and reduce. The best waste is waste never made.
  2. Reuse and repair. Keep things in use for longer.
  3. Recycle. Sort materials so they can be made into something new.
  4. Compost. Food and garden waste becomes fertiliser.
  5. Seal old dumps. Cap them with clay and soil, and plant on top.

Question 3

The effect of changing our practices

Five changes. For each one: what changes, what improves, and what it costs.

Question 3: Natural materials in agriculture

Swap chemicals for natural materials

Natural materials can do the job of synthetic fertiliser and pesticide, and they build up the soil as they work.

What changes

  • Compost and manure replace synthetic fertiliser and feed the soil slowly.
  • Cover crops protect bare soil over winter and hold on to nutrients.
  • Legumes host bacteria that fix nitrogen from the air.
  • Push-pull planting: Desmodium repels stemborer moths from maize, and Napier grass lures them away.

Effect of this change

0%

less nitrate leached when a non-legume cover crop replaced bare winter soil, with no significant change in yield. Tonitto 2006

What improves
Richer soil and cleaner rivers. Farmers using push-pull report maize yields about 20% higher. icipe
What it costs
More labour. Overused manure still pollutes, and Napier grass can spread in wet areas.
Question 3: Organic agriculture

Farm with the ecosystem

One crop, year after year, drains the soil and feeds its pests. Organic farms use variety and natural predators instead.

What changes

  • No synthetic fertiliser or pesticide.
  • Crop rotation breaks pest cycles and restores nutrients.
  • Natural pest control: hedges shelter ladybirds, which eat aphids. Spraying approved products is the last resort.

Effect of this change

What improves
About 30% more species on organic farms on average. Tuck 2014
What it costs
Yields about 19% lower, which can mean more land for the same food. Rotation and multi-cropping cut that gap to 8–9%. Ponisio 2015
Effect of ending a pesticide

How poison climbs a food chain

  1. A spray reaches the water. DDT measured just 0.000003 parts per million in nearby water, and plankton took it in. It stays in their fat.
  2. It concentrates at each step. Each animal eats many others and keeps their DDT. This is biomagnification.
  3. Top predators pay the price. Fish-eating birds in one study carried up to 75 ppm. DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey.
  4. Changing the practice worked. After the US banned most uses in 1972, bald eagles rose from 417 nesting pairs in 1963 to 9,789 by 2007.

Question 3: Conservation methods

Protect what's left, then connect it

The ridge never lost its old forest. It still holds the seeds and animals needed to refill the valley, so it gets protected first.

What changes

  • Protected areas work best when local people help run them.
  • Wildlife corridors like this green bridge let separated animals meet and breed, which restores gene flow.
  • Paying landowners makes protecting nature worth more than clearing it.
  • Seed banks and captive breeding keep a backup of species at risk.

Effect of this change

What improves
Costa Rica went from about a quarter forest in the 1980s to over half today. IIED A beaver trial in England found 37% more fish in dammed pools, and lower flood peaks. River Otter study
What it costs
Land is taken out of use, payments need funding, and beaver dams can flood farmland.
Question 3: Education

Teach it, or it won't last

Reed beds silt up and hedges get ripped out if nobody knows why they matter. A plan lasts when local people understand it.

What changes

  • School gardens teach composting and soil care with real results.
  • Farmer-to-farmer training: people trust a method once it works on a neighbour's land.
  • Citizen science: students survey birds and river insects, and their records become useful data.

Effect of this change

What improves
People look after what they understand, so restoration lasts. Costa Rica's recovery is partly credited to its education system. IPS/FAO
What it costs
Results are slow and hard to measure, and knowledge alone doesn't always change behaviour. Hands-on projects work better.
Question 3: Monitoring strategies

Measure it, or you're guessing

Monitoring shows whether a method is working in time to change it. If it isn't working, the plan changes.

What changes

  • River sensors measure oxygen and nitrate around the clock.
  • Drones and satellites map how much forest has returned.
  • Camera traps show which animals use the green bridge.
  • Bioindicators: mayfly larvae are sensitive to pollution, so many of them suggest a healthy stream. Save the Sound

Predict: the buoy shows nitrate spiking after every rainstorm. What should you do first?

Effect of this change

What improves
Problems are caught early, and money goes to methods proven to work. This is called adaptive management.
What it costs
Equipment and staff time. Data only helps if someone acts on it, and one indicator alone can mislead.

Our answers

Question 1Conserving and restoring natural resources

  • Forests: replant native mixes and protect regrowth.
  • Soil: contour ploughing, terraces, mulch and less tillage.
  • Water: restore wetlands, plant buffer strips and use drip irrigation.
  • Energy and minerals: use solar and wind, and recycle metals.

Question 2Reducing pollution

  • Air: scrubbers, clean energy and cleaner transport.
  • Water: treat waste before it reaches rivers, and clean up past pollution.
  • Land: reduce, reuse, recycle and compost, then seal old dumps.

Question 3The effect of changing our practices

  • Natural materials: cleaner rivers and richer soil, for more labour.
  • Organic agriculture: more species, for lower yields.
  • Conservation methods: habitats recover, but land is set aside.
  • Education: restoration lasts, but slowly.
  • Monitoring: problems are caught early, if people act on the data.
